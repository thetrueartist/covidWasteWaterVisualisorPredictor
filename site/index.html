        How this works

        Reading the levels

        Infected people shed SARS-CoV-2, flu and RSV into sewage, often before they feel ill and whether or not they test. Treatment works sample incoming sewage and measure each virus's genetic material, so wastewater shows community spread without relying on testing.

        Each area is compared with its own previous two years, separately for each virus. Units differ between countries and labs, so a level here means where this week sits in that area's recent history. After a long decline, "very low" means low for the last two years, not necessarily as low as ever. A reading of zero means the virus was below the lab's detection limit, not that there was none.

        The newest week is provisional: publishers often add samples and revise it, typically by around 10%.

        Where a country doesn't publish flu or RSV in wastewater (Scotland), the site uses its laboratory surveillance instead and says so. Lab data depends on who gets tested and lags wastewater by a few days.

          The forecast

          Each virus has its own gradient-boosted models, trained on every area that publishes that virus. They look at how fast each area's level has been changing, and at the trend across its whole country. They predict a range for each of the next six weeks, not a single number. Which inputs each virus's model uses was chosen by testing the options on two separate held-out years. Published data runs one to two weeks behind, so part of the forecast covers weeks that have already started.

          Where the model did no better than "assume nothing changes" in back-testing, that country's forecast uses no change instead. The table re-makes the past year's forecasts using today's data. Every forecast the site publishes is also saved and checked once the real data arrives: that's the live track record.

          What it can't tell you

          Wastewater tracks community trends, not the risk of any single outing. Small treatment works give noisy readings. The advice here follows general public-health practice. It is not medical advice. If you're unwell, stay home. If you're at high risk, follow your clinician's guidance.
